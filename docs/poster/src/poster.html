Software Defined Radio data processing on board OPS-SAT Space Laboratory

Marcin Jasiukowicz
Gdańsk University of Technology, Poland

Mission Background

OPS-SAT 1 satellite, launched in 2019, is ESA's first mission specifically designed as a flying laboratory for testing and demonstrating cutting-edge space technologies and operational concepts. The mission boasts a unique architecture by joining cutting-edge Commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) hardware and redundant design ensuring safety of operations at any time.

The purpose of the mission is to provide a safe and reconfigurable platform for in-orbit experimentation. OPS-SAT allows for testing of new software, and operational techniques in a real space environment without jeopardizing larger, more expensive missions. To accelerate the development and validation of future space technologies, OPS-SAT enables rapid prototyping and in-flight testing, helping mature promising technologies, like high-speed communication protocols, and innovative mission operations software, for future ESA missions.

OPS-SAT Space Laboratory

The satellite is a 3 unit CubeSat, measuring 30x10x10 cm. The satellite has two deployable solar panel arrays on its side. The overall systems architecture of the satellite is split into the Satellite Bus and the Payload section. To ensure robustness and mission success, OPS-SAT incorporates redundancy in critical systems. The spacecraft is designed to be highly fault-tolerant, capable of withstanding errors in experimental software and even hardware failures. The architecture continuously monitors for anomalies and can automatically initiate recovery procedures to ensure the satellite's survival.

At the heart of OPS-SAT, the Satellite Experimental Processing Platform integrates a Hard Processor System (HPS) with an FPGA (Field Programmable Gate Array) fabric. The HPS includes a dual-core ARM Cortex-A9 800 MHz CPU with 1 GB RAM. The computing platform is paired with 16 GB of eMMC memory used for persistent storage.

The HPS runs GNU/Linux Operating System allowing for a great variety of modern software to be run. The OS comes with a set of tools and abstractions known to developers working in many industries outside of the space sector. The available resources allow for high-level programming languages like Python and Java to be run. The FPGA component is reconfigurable, meaning it can be reprogrammed in-flight to adapt to different experiments and processing needs. Additionally, many of the signal lines and buses connecting the various subsystems have been connected to the fabric.

The SDR on OPS-SAT is an experimental radio receiver based on Lime Microsystems LMS6002D chip, capable of processing a wide range of frequencies from 300 MHz to 3 GHz with an adjustable sampling rate from 750 kHz to 20 MHz. The SDR frontend shares one of the antennas used with the Nanocom subsystems, tuned to the same frequency. This effectively limits the operational range to the UHF 70 cm band at around 400 MHz. The frontend includes an optional low pass filter tuned at 1.575 GHz allowing for filtering out more powerful signals. After the filter, a Low Noise Amplifier (LNA) is placed. It provides a gain of approximately 20 dB for the UHF band. Overall the system achieves -98 dBm sensitivity. The SDR is connected to the SEPP FPGA via a 12-bit parallel bus, allowing it to capture and buffer in-phase and quadrature (IQ) samples of the received Radio Frequency (RF) signal. The samples can be used by the HPS using direct memory access (DMA).

Challenges and Opportunities

Over its four years in orbit, OPS-SAT has developed specific operational characteristics, some of which have evolved due to the satellite's gradual degradation, while others have improved thanks to the development of new tools that facilitate a variety of repetitive procedures.

Degradation of eMMC

Over time, the SEPP's eMMC memory in OPS-SAT began to degrade due to radiation exposure and frequent use. This caused memory corruption and failures, limiting the system's ability to store and retrieve data. To address this, the Mission Control Team implemented a workaround using volatile RAM storage for temporary operations and developed custom memory mapping techniques to use non-corrupted portions of the eMMC. These solutions extended the operational life of the memory, though with reduced capacity.

SDR Streaming Interface (SIDLOC)

The SIDLOC experiment, developed by the Libre Space Foundation, required continuous IQ data streaming from the OPS-SAT's SDR subsystem. To achieve this, a custom FPGA implementation was created, incorporating Scatter-Gather DMA and a Dual Clock FIFO buffer, which allowed for continuous streaming of radio data. Although the experiment still relied on downlinking raw data for processing on the ground, it paved the way for real-time, high-bandwidth SDR operations on board small satellites.

SpaceWire

The integration of SpaceWire protocol between the SEPP and the CCSDS Engine allowed for high-speed data transfer within OPS-SAT. Initially, communication was limited to slower CAN Bus, but post-launch, SpaceWire was deployed over a previously unused LVDS line, enabling Ethernet-like connectivity for remote management of the satellite’s processing platform. This upgrade drastically improved data throughput and allowed for faster software updates and file transfers, enhancing the overall flexibility and responsiveness of the mission.

SDR Experiment Implementation

As OPS-SAT's operational constraints evolved, the SDR subsystem faced limitations, initially due to the DMA interface and later from CPU degradation, preventing real-time data processing. To revive SDR experiments, a set of tools and procedures was developed to overcome these issues, making it easier for the Mission Control Team to operate the subsystem. With the decay of OPS-SAT's orbit affecting other experiments, optimizing SDR operations became even more crucial. The experiment was organized into distinct "actions," each addressing specific functions within the experiment lifecycle:

Record

The Record action focused on capturing radio signals using the SDR subsystem on OPS-SAT. To overcome the limited memory due to the degradation of the SEPP, the recorded samples were directly written to a specific eMMC partition via a modified SDR driver. This approach bypassed the constrained operational memory, but relied on stable portions of the degraded eMMC for storage. The TAR file format has been used for easier data management in the raw memory.

Waterfall

The Waterfall action generated a waterfall plot to visually represent the captured radio spectrum. This allowed operators to quickly identify signals of interest in the recorded data, addressing the challenge of efficiently handling large amounts of raw data. By generating a preview onboard, operators could make informed decisions on which parts of the spectrum to downlink, reducing the volume of data transmitted to Earth and optimizing the limited bandwidth in the SDR streaming interface.

Downsampling

The Downsample action was implemented to extract and focus on specific signal frequencies within the captured wideband data. By reducing the sampling rate for certain signals of interest, the file size was drastically reduced, addressing both storage limitations on the eMMC and downlink constraints. This action provided a critical opportunity to process and manage SDR data more effectively, ensuring that only the most relevant data was transmitted back to Earth.

Results

A live demonstration taking place at the 37th Chaos Communication Congress (37C3), one of the leading cybersecurity events in Europe. The test gathered a small audience of fellow amateur radio enthusiasts and members of the Libre Space Foundation, who have developed the Sidloc FPGA image used in this setup. The voice transmission started exactly on December 29th, 2023 at 19:03:38 UTC, the moment the experiment was scheduled on board OPS-SAT.

The message included greetings from earth and respective amateur radio callsigns of the operators present. Another transmission has been conducted in parallel including an QSL card transmitted via Slow Scan Television (SSTV) encoding.

The downlink was completed over the S-Band channel in the evening, morning, and next day's evening satellite passes. Each pass another chunk of the recording was available and the transmission could be decoded, confirming the link budget margin was indeed enough to detect the signal on board. The decoded voice signal was quite faint, but enough to distinguish the transmitted message, especially with prior knowledge of its content. The SSTV transmission could not be decoded.

With the full recording on ground, later processing and development of the experiment was conducted on the Engineering Model of OPS-SAT. Just like on the real spacecraft, the recording was loaded into the eMMC in the same fashion as if it would be recorded via SDR. Then the down-sampling action of the experiment could be applied, extracting the transmission at given parameters. The resulting file weighed around 9 MB. If it were conducted on board, it could be downloaded in about the same ground-station time as the waterfall preview of the entire recording.

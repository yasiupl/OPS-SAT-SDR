Software Defined Radio data processing on board OPS-SAT Space Laboratory

Marcin Jasiukowicz
Gdańsk University of Technology, Poland

Mission Background

OPS-SAT 1 satel­lite, launched in 2019, is ESA's first mis­sion specif­i­cally designed as a fly­ing lab­o­ra­tory for test­ing and demon­strat­ing cut­ting-edge space tech­nolo­gies and oper­a­tional con­cepts. The mis­sion boasts a unique archi­tec­ture by join­ing cut­ting-edge Commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) hard­ware and redun­dant design ensur­ing safety of oper­a­tions at any time.

The pur­pose of the mis­sion is to pro­vide a safe and recon­fig­urable plat­form for in-orbit exper­i­men­ta­tion. OPS-SAT allows for test­ing of new soft­ware, and oper­a­tional tech­niques in a real space envi­ron­ment with­out jeop­ar­diz­ing larger, more expen­sive mis­sions. To accel­er­ate the devel­op­ment and val­i­da­tion of future space tech­nolo­gies, OPS-SAT enables rapid pro­to­typ­ing and in-flight test­ing, help­ing mature promis­ing tech­nolo­gies, like high-speed com­mu­ni­ca­tion pro­to­cols, and inno­va­tive mis­sion oper­a­tions soft­ware, for future ESA mis­sions.

OPS-SAT Space Laboratory

The satel­lite is a 3 unit CubeSat, mea­sur­ing 30x10x10 cm. The satel­lite has two deploy­able solar panel arrays on its side. The over­all sys­tems archi­tec­ture of the satel­lite is split into the Satellite Bus and the Payload sec­tion. To ensure robust­ness and mis­sion suc­cess, OPS-SAT incor­po­rates redun­dancy in crit­i­cal sys­tems. The space­craft is designed to be highly fault-tol­er­ant, capa­ble of with­stand­ing errors in exper­i­men­tal soft­ware and even hard­ware fail­ures. The archi­tec­ture con­tin­u­ously mon­i­tors for anom­alies and can auto­mat­i­cally ini­ti­ate recov­ery pro­ce­dures to ensure the satel­lite's sur­vival.

At the heart of OPS-SAT, the Satellite Experimental Processing Platform inte­grates a Hard Processor System (HPS) with an FPGA (Field Programmable Gate Array) fab­ric. The HPS includes a dual-core ARM Cortex-A9 800 MHz CPU with 1 GB RAM. The com­put­ing plat­form is paired with 16 GB of eMMC mem­ory used for per­sis­tent stor­age.

The HPS runs GNU/Linux Operating System allow­ing for a great vari­ety of mod­ern soft­ware to be run. The OS comes with a set of tools and abstrac­tions known to devel­op­ers work­ing in many indus­tries out­side of the space sec­tor. The avail­able resources allow for high-level pro­gram­ming lan­guages like Python and Java to be run. The FPGA com­po­nent is recon­fig­urable, mean­ing it can be repro­grammed in-flight to adapt to dif­fer­ent exper­i­ments and pro­cess­ing needs. Addi­tion­ally, many of the sig­nal lines and buses con­nect­ing the var­i­ous sub­sys­tems have been con­nected to the fab­ric.

The SDR on OPS-SAT is an exper­i­men­tal radio receiver based on Lime Microsystems LMS6002D chip, capa­ble of pro­cess­ing a wide range of fre­quen­cies from 300 MHz to 3 GHz with an adjustable sam­pling rate from 750 kHz to 20 MHz. The SDR fron­tend shares one of the anten­nas used with the Nanocom sub­sys­tems, tuned to the same fre­quency. This effec­tively lim­its the oper­a­tional range to the UHF 70 cm band at around 400 MHz. The fron­tend includes an optional low pass fil­ter tuned at 1.575 GHz allow­ing for fil­ter­ing out more pow­er­ful sig­nals. After the fil­ter, a Low Noise Amplifier (LNA) is placed. It pro­vides a gain of approx­i­mately 20 dB for the UHF band. Over­all the sys­tem achieves -98 dBm sen­si­tiv­ity. The SDR is con­nected to the SEPP FPGA via a 12-bit par­al­lel bus, allow­ing it to cap­ture and buf­fer in-phase and quad­ra­ture (IQ) sam­ples of the received Radio Frequency (RF) sig­nal. The sam­ples can be used by the HPS using direct mem­ory access (DMA).

Challenges and Opportunities

Over its four years in orbit, OPS-SAT has devel­oped spe­cific oper­a­tional char­ac­ter­is­tics, some of which have evolved due to the satel­lite's grad­ual degra­da­tion, while oth­ers have improved thanks to the devel­op­ment of new tools that facil­i­tate a vari­ety of repet­i­tive pro­ce­dures.

Degradation of eMMC

Over time, the SEPP's eMMC mem­ory in OPS-SAT began to degrade due to radi­a­tion expo­sure and fre­quent use. This caused mem­ory cor­rup­tion and fail­ures, lim­it­ing the sys­tem's abil­ity to store and retrieve data. To address this, the Mission Control Team imple­mented a work­around using volatile RAM stor­age for tem­po­rary oper­a­tions and devel­oped cus­tom mem­ory map­ping tech­niques to use non-cor­rupted por­tions of the eMMC. These solu­tions extended the oper­a­tional life of the mem­ory, though with reduced capac­ity.

SDR Streaming Interface (SIDLOC)

The SIDLOC exper­i­ment, devel­oped by the Libre Space Foundation, required con­tin­u­ous IQ data stream­ing from the OPS-SAT's SDR sub­sys­tem. To achieve this, a cus­tom FPGA imple­men­ta­tion was cre­ated, incor­po­rat­ing Scatter-Gather DMA and a Dual Clock FIFO buf­fer, which allowed for con­tin­u­ous stream­ing of radio data. Although the exper­i­ment still relied on down­link­ing raw data for pro­cess­ing on the ground, it paved the way for real-time, high-band­width SDR oper­a­tions on board small satel­lites.

SpaceWire

The inte­gra­tion of SpaceWire pro­to­col between the SEPP and the CCSDS Engine allowed for high-speed data trans­fer within OPS-SAT. Ini­tially, com­mu­ni­ca­tion was lim­ited to slower CAN Bus, but post-launch, SpaceWire was deployed over a pre­vi­ously unused LVDS line, enabling Ethernet-like con­nec­tiv­ity for remote man­age­ment of the satel­lite’s pro­cess­ing plat­form. This upgrade dras­ti­cally improved data through­put and allowed for faster soft­ware updates and file trans­fers, enhanc­ing the over­all flex­i­bil­ity and respon­sive­ness of the mis­sion.

SDR Experiment Implementation

As OPS-SAT's oper­a­tional con­straints evolved, the SDR sub­sys­tem faced lim­i­ta­tions, ini­tially due to the DMA inter­face and later from CPU degra­da­tion, pre­vent­ing real-time data pro­cess­ing. To revive SDR exper­i­ments, a set of tools and pro­ce­dures was devel­oped to over­come these issues, mak­ing it eas­ier for the Mission Control Team to oper­ate the sub­sys­tem. With the decay of OPS-SAT's orbit affect­ing other exper­i­ments, opti­miz­ing SDR oper­a­tions became even more cru­cial. The exper­i­ment was orga­nized into dis­tinct "actions," each address­ing spe­cific func­tions within the exper­i­ment life­cy­cle:

Record

The Record action focused on cap­tur­ing radio sig­nals using the SDR sub­sys­tem on OPS-SAT. To over­come the lim­ited mem­ory due to the degra­da­tion of the SEPP, the recorded sam­ples were directly writ­ten to a spe­cific eMMC par­ti­tion via a mod­i­fied SDR driver. This approach bypassed the con­strained oper­a­tional mem­ory, but relied on sta­ble por­tions of the degraded eMMC for stor­age. The TAR file for­mat has been used for eas­ier data man­age­ment in the raw mem­ory.

Waterfall

The Waterfall action gen­er­ated a water­fall plot to vis­ually rep­re­sent the cap­tured radio spec­trum. This allowed oper­a­tors to quickly iden­tify sig­nals of inter­est in the recorded data, address­ing the chal­lenge of effi­ciently han­dling large amounts of raw data. By gen­er­at­ing a pre­view onboard, oper­a­tors could make informed deci­sions on which parts of the spec­trum to down­link, reduc­ing the vol­ume of data trans­mit­ted to Earth and opti­miz­ing the lim­ited band­width in the SDR stream­ing inter­face.

Downsampling

The Downsample action was imple­mented to extract and focus on spe­cific sig­nal fre­quen­cies within the cap­tured wide­band data. By reduc­ing the sam­pling rate for cer­tain sig­nals of inter­est, the file size was dras­ti­cally reduced, address­ing both stor­age lim­i­ta­tions on the eMMC and down­link con­straints. This action pro­vided a crit­i­cal oppor­tu­nity to process and man­age SDR data more effec­tively, ensur­ing that only the most rel­e­vant data was trans­mit­ted back to Earth.

Results

A live demon­stra­tion tak­ing place at the 37th Chaos Communication Congress (37C3), one of the lead­ing cyber­se­cu­rity events in Europe. The test gath­ered a small audi­ence of fel­low ama­teur radio enthu­si­asts and mem­bers of the Libre Space Foundation, who have devel­oped the Sidloc FPGA image used in this setup. The voice trans­mis­sion started exactly on December 29th, 2023 at 19:03:38 UTC, the moment the exper­i­ment was sched­uled on board OPS-SAT.

The mes­sage included greet­ings from earth and respec­tive ama­teur radio call­signs of the oper­a­tors present. Another trans­mis­sion has been con­ducted in par­al­lel includ­ing an QSL card trans­mit­ted via Slow Scan Television (SSTV) encod­ing.

The down­link was com­pleted over the S-Band chan­nel in the evening, morn­ing, and next day's evening satel­lite passes. Each pass another chunk of the record­ing was avail­able and the trans­mis­sion could be decoded, con­firm­ing the link bud­get mar­gin was indeed enough to detect the sig­nal on board. The decoded voice sig­nal was quite faint, but enough to dis­tin­guish the trans­mit­ted mes­sage, espe­cially with prior knowl­edge of its con­tent. The SSTV trans­mis­sion could not be decoded.

With the full record­ing on ground, later pro­cess­ing and devel­op­ment of the exper­i­ment was con­ducted on the Engineering Model of OPS-SAT. Just like on the real space­craft, the record­ing was loaded into the eMMC in the same fash­ion as if it would be recorded via SDR. Then the down-sam­pling action of the exper­i­ment could be applied, extract­ing the trans­mis­sion at given param­e­ters. The result­ing file weighed around 9 MB. If it were con­ducted on board, it could be down­loaded in about the same ground-sta­tion time as the water­fall pre­view of the entire record­ing.
